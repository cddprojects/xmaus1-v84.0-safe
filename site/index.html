A guide for Australians aged 25 to 65

Make sense of remote and flexible work in Australia

Maybe you’re changing careers. Maybe you’re returning to work after time at home, juggling family, or planning a gentler later chapter. Either way, the words in job ads can be confusing.

This guide explains what remote, online and flexible work actually mean, what employers tend to ask for, and what to check before you apply. It won’t promise you a job. It will help you read advertisements with more confidence.

Start with the differences

Job ads use these words loosely, so one employer’s “remote” can be another’s “hybrid”. Use these definitions as a starting point, then read the ad itself.

Remote work

You do the job away from an employer’s office, usually from home. Some remote roles let you live anywhere in Australia. Others need you to live in a particular state.

Online work

The work itself happens through a computer or phone, such as tutoring, customer support or bookkeeping. It may be an employee role, or it may be contract or freelance work.

Flexible work

You have some say over when or how much you work, such as part-time hours, school-hours shifts or adjusted start times. Flexible work can still be done at a workplace.

Where they overlap

A job can be all three at once, like a part-time bookkeeping role you do from home on hours you agree with your employer. Or it can be just one. A flexible retail job is still in a shop, and a remote job can have fixed hours.

So it helps to ask two separate questions. Where will I work? And when will I work?

A quick word on hybrid

Hybrid means a mix of home and workplace days. Some employers set the days. Others let teams choose.

For example: an ad says “hybrid, three days in the office”. That’s very different from a fully remote role, even though both might mention working from home.

Your next step: write down which matters more to you right now, where you work or when you work. You’ll use that as you read the sections below.

Picture the work behind the job title

Job titles don’t always tell you much. These are examples only. Real tasks, skills and arrangements depend on the employer and the advertisement.

Example role

Customer service officer

Tasks may include: answering calls, emails or chats, updating customer records, solving billing questions.

Useful skills: clear writing, patience, comfort learning new systems.

Arrangements you might see: rostered shifts, sometimes evenings or weekends, with training done in person or online.

Example role

Bookkeeper

Tasks may include: entering invoices, reconciling accounts, preparing payroll or BAS information for review.

Useful skills: accuracy, accounting software, confidentiality.

Arrangements you might see: part-time or contract, often with deadlines rather than set hours.

Example role

Administration or virtual assistant

Tasks may include: managing calendars and inboxes, booking appointments, preparing documents.

Useful skills: organisation, email and spreadsheet confidence, discretion.

Arrangements you might see: part-time hours, a mix of set and flexible times, sometimes working with one client.

Example role

Online tutor

Tasks may include: running video lessons, preparing materials, giving feedback to students or parents.

Useful skills: subject knowledge, explaining clearly, reliable internet.

Arrangements you might see: after-school and weekend sessions, often casual or contract. Check what checks or registrations apply.

Example role

Content or copywriter

Tasks may include: writing web pages, newsletters or product descriptions, editing drafts.

Useful skills: clear writing, research, taking feedback well.

Arrangements you might see: project-based or part-time work, often with a portfolio or writing sample requested.

Example role

Public service officer

Tasks may include: assessing applications, answering enquiries, writing correspondence, supporting a team.

Useful skills: following procedures, plain writing, handling sensitive information.

Arrangements you might see: flexible working options vary by agency and role, and some roles need an office or site presence.

Start with the skills you already use

Many people rule themselves out too soon. Everyday work, volunteering and running a household can build skills employers look for. What counts is how the ad words it.

Experience

Ads often ask for “experience in a similar role”. Think about tasks you’ve done rather than titles you’ve held. Organising a school fundraiser, for example, involves budgeting, emailing and coordinating people.

Qualifications

Some roles need a specific qualification, licence or registration, such as a teaching or accounting credential. Many others don’t. If an ad lists one, check whether it’s required or just preferred.

Software knowledge

Look for named tools like Microsoft Office, Google Workspace, Xero, MYOB or a customer system. Employers sometimes train you on their own systems. Basic email, spreadsheets and video calls are a common starting point.

Communication

Remote work leans heavily on writing, because you’ll often explain things by email or chat instead of in person. Clear, polite, to-the-point messages are valuable.

Equipment and set-up

Ads may mention a reliable internet connection, a computer that meets certain specifications, a headset or a quiet space. Some employers provide equipment. Others expect you to supply your own. Ask what’s included before you accept.

Essential or desirable?

Ads usually separate what they must have from what would be a bonus. Wording gives clues, though it varies between employers.

Check how the role would fit your week

“Flexible” can mean very different things. Before you apply, picture an ordinary week and see where the job would land.

Hours and shifts

Are the hours fixed, rostered or self-managed? Do they include evenings, weekends or public holidays? School-hours work usually means set times, so check the ad lists them.

Meetings and availability

Some remote roles expect you to be online at set times for calls or team meetings. Others are measured by results. Check which applies and what time zone is used.

Office attendance

A role advertised as remote or flexible may still need occasional days at a workplace for training, team days or client visits. Ask how often, and what the travel looks like.

Location restrictions

Some employers hire only from certain states or need you close to a site. A role listed under a city with “remote” may still need you to live in that state.

Pay

Check whether pay is hourly or a salary, whether superannuation is included, and whether penalty rates or allowances apply. Be cautious about pay that’s described only as “up to” or based on commission.

Employment arrangement

Employee, casual, fixed-term, contractor and freelancer are different arrangements. They affect leave, super, tax and job security. If you’re unsure what applies to you, the Fair Work Ombudsman has plain-English information.

Weighing up a “flexible” role

Say an ad offers 20 hours a week, work from home, “flexible hours”. Looking closer, the employer needs the person online between 9 am and 1 pm on weekdays. For someone with school pick-ups, that could work well. For someone who wanted to choose their own hours, it might not. The ad was accurate. The fit is what mattered.

A few questions you might still have

Do I need experience in remote work?

Not always. Many employers care more about the skills you’d use in the job, like communication, organisation and reliability. Check whether the ad asks for remote experience specifically.

Do I need a qualification?

It depends on the role. Some, like teaching, accounting or health roles, may need particular qualifications or registration. Others value experience instead. The ad should say what’s required.

What equipment will I need?

Commonly a computer, a reliable internet connection and a phone or headset. Some employers supply equipment, and some expect you to use your own. Ask before you accept.

How flexible will the job really be?

It varies a lot. Flexible might mean choosing your hours, or it might mean fixed part-time hours at home. Check the hours, meetings and attendance expectations in the ad, and ask if they’re not clear.

Where do I apply?

Apply through the employer’s own website or the method named in the ad, such as a job board listing that links to the employer. Be careful with offers that come to you unprompted or ask for payment.
