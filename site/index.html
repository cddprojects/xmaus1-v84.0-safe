A guide for Australians

Make sense of remote and flexible work in Australia

Changing careers, heading back to work, fitting a job around family, or easing into a gentler chapter? You’re in good company. The words used to describe this kind of work just aren’t always clear.

Remote, online and flexible work sound alike, but they answer different questions. We’ll show you how they differ, where they overlap, and what to check before you decide. No big promises. Just clear, honest help.

01 · Work types

Start with the differences

One employer’s “remote” can be another’s “hybrid”, so it helps to know what each word usually means. Then you can read between the lines.

Remote work

You do the job away from the employer’s office, usually from home. Some remote roles let you live anywhere in Australia. Others need you to live in a certain state.

Online work

The work happens through a computer or phone, like tutoring, customer support or bookkeeping. It could be an employee role, or contract or freelance work.

Flexible work

You have some say in when or how much you work, such as part-time hours, school-hours shifts or adjusted start times. Flexible work can still be at a workplace.

Where they overlap

A job can be all three at once, like a part-time bookkeeping role you do from home on hours you agree with your employer. Or it can be just one. A flexible retail job is still in a shop, and a remote job can have fixed hours.

So try asking two separate questions. Where will I work? And when will I work?

A quick word on hybrid

Hybrid is a mix of home days and workplace days. Some employers set the days. Others let teams decide.

For example: “hybrid, three days in the office” is quite different from a fully remote role, even if both mention working from home.

02 · Role examples

Picture the work behind the job title

A job title doesn’t always tell you what your day looks like. These are examples only. Real tasks, skills and arrangements depend on the employer. Many roles can be more than one type, so each example shows how it’s often described.

Example role

Customer service officer

Often described as: Remote · Flexible

A day might include

Answering calls, emails or chats, updating customer records and sorting out billing questions.

Handy skills

Clear writing, patience, being comfortable learning new systems.

Arrangements you might see

Rostered shifts, sometimes evenings or weekends, with training in person or online.

Example role

Bookkeeper

Often described as: Remote · Online · Flexible

A day might include

Entering invoices, reconciling accounts and preparing payroll or BAS information for review.

Handy skills

Accuracy, accounting software, keeping things confidential.

Arrangements you might see

Part-time or contract work, often tied to deadlines rather than set hours.

Example role

Administration or virtual assistant

Often described as: Remote · Online · Flexible

A day might include

Managing calendars and inboxes, booking appointments and preparing documents.

Handy skills

Organisation, email and spreadsheet confidence, discretion.

Arrangements you might see

Part-time hours, a mix of set and flexible times, sometimes supporting one client.

Example role

Online tutor

Often described as: Online · Flexible

A day might include

Running video lessons, preparing materials and giving feedback to students or parents.

Handy skills

Subject knowledge, explaining things clearly, reliable internet.

Arrangements you might see

After-school and weekend sessions, often casual or contract. Check which checks or registrations apply.

Example role

Content writer or copywriter

Often described as: Remote · Online · Flexible

A day might include

Writing web pages, newsletters or product descriptions, and editing drafts.

Handy skills

Clear writing, research, taking feedback well.

Arrangements you might see

Project-based or part-time work, often with a portfolio or writing sample requested.

Example role

Public service officer

Often described as: Hybrid · Flexible · Sometimes remote

A day might include

Assessing applications, answering enquiries, writing letters and supporting a team.

Handy skills

Following procedures, plain writing, handling sensitive information with care.

Arrangements you might see

Flexible options vary by agency and role, and some roles need an office or site presence.

03 · Requirements

Start with the skills you already use

So many people rule themselves out too soon. Work, volunteering and running a household all build skills employers look for. It’s often just a matter of how employers word them.

Your everyday experience, in employer language

Organised the school fundraiserBudgeting, coordinating people, clear email communication
Ran the household budget and billsRecord keeping, attention to detail, working with numbers
Looked after a family member’s appointmentsScheduling, liaising with services, handling sensitive matters
Years on the shop floor or phonesCustomer service, problem solving, working under pressure

These show how to think about your experience. Whether it counts depends on the employer and the role.

Experience

Employers often ask for “experience in a similar role”. Think about the tasks you’ve done, not only the titles you’ve held.

Qualifications

Some roles need a specific qualification, licence or registration, such as teaching or accounting. Many don’t. If a role lists one, check whether it’s required or just preferred.

Software knowledge

You might see Microsoft Office, Google Workspace, Xero, MYOB or a customer system named. Some employers train you on their own tools. Email, spreadsheets and video calls are a common starting point.

Communication

Remote work leans on writing, because you’ll often explain things by email or chat. Clear, kind, to-the-point messages are a real strength.

Equipment and set-up

Employers may mention reliable internet, a computer that meets certain specifications, a headset or a quiet space. Some employers provide equipment and others expect you to use your own. It’s fair to ask what’s included before you accept.

  • Computer
  • Reliable internet
  • Headset
  • Quiet space

Essential or desirable?

Employers usually separate what they must have from what would be a bonus. The wording gives you clues, though it varies between employers.

Essential

  • “Must have”, “required”, “mandatory”
  • A licence, registration or check the law requires
  • The right to work in Australia
  • Availability for the stated hours or shifts

Desirable

  • “Desirable”, “preferred”, “advantageous”
  • Experience with a particular software brand
  • A related qualification, when experience is also accepted
  • Experience in the same industry

Your next stepTake a role you like, list what it asks for, and mark each requirement as essential, desirable or unclear. If something’s unclear, it’s fine to ask the employer.

04 · Practical considerations

Check how the role would fit your week

“Flexible” can mean very different things. Before you apply, picture an ordinary week and see where the job would land.

Hours and shifts

Are the hours fixed, rostered or self-managed? Do they include evenings, weekends or public holidays? School-hours work usually means set times, so check the hours are stated.

Meetings and availability

Some remote roles expect you to be online at set times for calls or team meetings. Others focus on results. Check which applies, and which time zone is used.

Office attendance

A role described as remote or flexible may still need the odd day at a workplace for training, team days or client visits. Ask how often, and what the travel looks like.

Location restrictions

Some employers hire only from certain states or need you near a site. A role listed under a city with “remote” may still need you to live in that state.

Pay

Check whether pay is hourly or a salary, whether super is included, and whether penalty rates or allowances apply. Be cautious if pay is only described as “up to” or is commission only.

Employment arrangement

Employee, casual, fixed-term, contractor and freelancer are different arrangements. They affect leave, super, tax and job security. The Fair Work Ombudsman has plain-English information if you’re unsure.

07 · FAQs

A few questions you might still have

Short answers to the questions we hear most. Details always depend on the employer.

Do I need experience in remote work?

Not always. Many employers care more about the skills you’d use in the job, like communication, organisation and reliability. Check whether the role asks for remote experience specifically.

Do I need a qualification?

It depends on the role. Teaching, accounting and health roles, for example, may need particular qualifications or registration. Others value experience instead. The role description should say what’s required.

What equipment will I need?

Commonly a computer, reliable internet and a phone or headset. Some employers supply equipment, and some expect you to use your own. It’s fine to ask before you accept.

How flexible will the job really be?

It varies a lot. Flexible might mean choosing your hours, or it might mean fixed part-time hours at home. Check the hours, meetings and attendance expectations in the role description, and ask if they’re not clear.

Where do I apply?

Through the employer’s own website, or the method the employer names, such as a job board listing that links to the employer. Be careful with offers that arrive out of the blue or ask for payment.
